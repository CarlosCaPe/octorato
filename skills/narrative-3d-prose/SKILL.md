# Narrative 3D Prose

## What it is

A first draft usually tells an idea once, from one side, in the order the writer thought of it. The reader gets the fact and nothing to hold. Call that **2D**.

The **3D** version keeps every fact and adds two things: more perspectives on the same idea, and detail the reader can picture. The model is a reference novelist (a contemporary Spanish literary novelist) who described his own craft in building and musical terms. Only his techniques transfer. His phrases, names, characters and scenes never do, and his name never appears in outward text.

## When to use it

| Use it for | Phase |
|---|---|
| Nonfiction book prose: memoir, business books, technical explanations of real events | Writing, editing, QA |
| Fiction: novels and short stories | Writing, editing, QA |
| A critic loop over a manuscript | QA (the VOZ-3D rubric below) |

Triggers in EN and ES: "narrative voice", "3D voice", "voz 3D", "more perspectives", "más perspectivas", "make it picturable", "hazlo imaginable".

In fiction the techniques apply directly: the "How, in nonfiction" lines become "how, in your story", and the guardrails below bind to the story's own established facts instead of the real record.

Not for: reference docs, API docs, step-by-step instructions, chat replies. An instruction the reader must follow stays flat and exact. 3D goes in the scene and the explanation, never inside the step.

This skill sits on top of a book's own voice guide. If a series already has one, that guide still rules register, vocabulary and legal limits; this skill adds the 3D layer.

## The techniques

Confidence comes from the research report: **high** = several sources agree, including the novelist's own interviews; **medium** = one source, or a reporter's paraphrase; **adaptation** = our extension, not something he taught.

### 1. Multi-angle entry: one idea, N doors (the 3D core)

- **Why.** One angle gives the reader a fact; three angles give them an object they can turn in the hand.
- **Confidence.** High that he described his cycle as one story broken into many angles with several entry doors. Using it to *explain* one idea from several sides is an **adaptation**.
- **How, in nonfiction.** Pick one concept or event. Show it from 2 to 7 real sides: the person who sent it, the person who received it, the buyer, the reader, the ledger, the clock. Announce the device once ("the first side is...") so the reader knows the game. Each side carries at least one real, sourced detail. Close by naming what all sides add up to, in one or two sentences, without repeating them.

### 2. Nested reveals

- **Why.** An answer that opens a new, smaller question keeps the reader turning pages.
- **Confidence.** High (he described his first cycle novel as Russian dolls, each mystery opening onto another).
- **How, in nonfiction.** Order the real facts so each one answers the last question and raises the next. Never manufacture suspense about a real outcome: if you already know it, you may delay it, never disguise it. The reveal must be a fact that really happened.

### 3. Cinematic staging

- **Why.** The reader builds the picture out of colors, movement and physical traits, the way a camera would show it.
- **Confidence.** High that he rebuilt the 19th-century novel with the grammar of cinema and named 1970s filmmakers as an influence. The specific "screenwriting technique" attribution comes from an article with no citation, so treat that detail as second-hand.
- **How, in nonfiction.** Give the scene a frame: a time on the clock, an object in a hand, a screen with a number on it. Use physical detail that a source supports (a photo, a recording, a log). When the detail is a picture you made up to help the reader, mark it as an image (see guardrails).

### 4. Musical rhythm and counterpoint

- **Why.** Rhythm carries mood. Contrast in length and tone creates the emotional beat.
- **Confidence.** Medium (one secondary source, likely translated, quotes him comparing his method to a composer using rhythm and counterpoint).
- **How, in nonfiction.** Vary sentence length on purpose: a long sentence that builds, then a short one that lands. Put a cold number next to a warm belief. Put a small detail next to a big claim. Read the paragraph aloud; if every sentence has the same length, rewrite one.

### 5. Place as a character

- **Why.** A place with a dramatic job (it helps, it blocks, it changes) is remembered better than a backdrop.
- **Confidence.** Medium (mostly a reporter's paraphrase of a 2010 talk).
- **How, in nonfiction.** The "place" can be a room, a city, a store page, a dashboard, a terminal window. Give it one trait the reader can see and one effect on the story. Keep it real: describe what the place actually showed.

### 6. Show through deeds and dialogue

- **Why.** A reader believes what a person does and says more than what the narrator asserts about them.
- **Confidence.** Medium, **split vote (2-1)**. The direct quote is about villains; the general rule is the reporter's paraphrase.
- **Not a ban on interiority.** His own narrators narrate inner life a lot. The rule is "don't only assert", not "never say what someone felt".
- **How, in nonfiction.** Quote the real message, the real line, the real click. Use verbatim quotes only from a source. If there is no source for what someone said, report the act, not invented dialogue.

### 7. Retrospective first-person narrator with foreshadowing

- **Why.** A narrator who looks back can frame memory and plant a line that pulls the reader forward.
- **Confidence.** Medium (one undergraduate thesis; the opening frame can be checked against the novel). The same source flags an abrupt shift in narrator position as a discordance.
- **How, in nonfiction.** Tell from the present, looking back. Foreshadow only with facts that really happened later ("that number would matter three weeks later" only if it did). Keep the narrator's position consistent; if you shift it, flag the shift on purpose.

### 8. Hybrid registers

- **Why.** Mixing registers (gothic, mystery, romance, humor, historical fiction, fable) keeps long prose alive, and the craft is in making them cohere.
- **Confidence.** High (his own words: the hard part is getting many registers to work together; a critic adds that the novelty is in the combination, not in new elements).
- **How, in nonfiction.** Adaptation: let a technical explanation borrow one register at a time: a beat of humor, a line of history, a fable-like image. One register per paragraph is plenty. The technical fact stays exact whatever register carries it.

Low-confidence note: one reader's blog reads the comic sidekick's cheek and half-believed folk sayings as a source of the humor. Treat that as a possible flavor, not a rule.

## Nonfiction guardrails (hard rules)

1. **Every fact stays real and sourced.** Dates, numbers, quotes, names, outcomes: each one traces to a source the editor can open. Keep the source next to the passage (for example an HTML comment that does not print).
2. **3D adds, it never invents.** It adds perspectives and sensory detail. It never adds events, quotes, numbers or outcomes.
3. **Images are marked as images.** A metaphor or a pictured scene that is not a fact is labeled in the source note as an image. In the prose, frame it so the reader can tell ("think of it as...", "picture...").
4. **A belief is the author's belief, never a promise.** "I believe X" is fine. "You will get X" is not, unless a source proves it for this reader.
5. **Narrator license is disclosed.** If a line puts a thought or reaction in a real person's mouth with no source, the editor flags it as a question to that person before it ships.

## Excess guards (checklist)

Several critics, a minority in a mostly favorable reception, flagged these excesses (medium confidence; the forced-metaphor point was verified only through a search snippet). Check each one before you call a passage done.

- [ ] **Melodrama.** Emotion bigger than the event that caused it.
- [ ] **Stock villains.** A person or company drawn as purely bad, with no real act shown.
- [ ] **Too much ominous atmosphere.** Shadows, dread and gloom stacked where the facts don't call for them.
- [ ] **Forced or stacked metaphors.** One image per idea. Two images in a sentence only when both are true and the sentence says so.
- [ ] **Brilliance next to cliché.** A sharp line followed by a worn phrase. Cut the worn phrase.
- [ ] **A long embedded document used to dump backstory.** A letter, log or report pasted in to deliver history. Quote the two lines that matter and tell the rest.
- [ ] **Cadence rules from `human-cadence`.** No em-dashes, no AI filler words, no forced triads, no paired contrast frame (rule 3 there), no rigid transitions, no repeated conclusions.

## QA rubric: family VOZ-3D

A critic agent runs this over a manuscript chapter by chapter. Each finding names the passage (file and line), the type, the severity and a one-line fix.

### Finding types

| Code | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **VOZ-3D-FLAT** | A technical term or key event told from one angle only, with nothing the reader can picture | "A token is a unit of text." and the paragraph moves on |
| **VOZ-3D-EXCESS** | A hit on the excess checklist above | Three metaphors in one sentence; gloom with no event behind it |
| **VOZ-3D-INVENTED** | Nonfiction: a detail added for texture with no source (an event, a quote, a number, an outcome). Fiction: a detail that contradicts the story's own established facts. Counts as a **fact-level failure**, not a style note | Nonfiction: "She smiled at the screen" when no source says she did. Fiction: a character seen in a city the story already placed elsewhere that day |

### Severity

| Severity | Rule |
|---|---|
| **high** | Any VOZ-3D-INVENTED. A VOZ-3D-FLAT on a concept or event the book depends on (a defined term, a turning point, a number the reader must remember). A VOZ-3D-EXCESS that changes what the reader believes happened (melodrama about a real outcome, a real person drawn as a stock villain) |
| **medium** | VOZ-3D-FLAT on a secondary concept or event. VOZ-3D-EXCESS that slows the reader or reads as purple, without distorting facts |
| **low** | Polish: a sentence-length run, one extra image, an atmosphere beat that could be cut |

### Exit criterion

A chapter, and the book, closes its critic loop only with **0 open VOZ-3D findings of severity high**. VOZ-3D-INVENTED is never downgraded: it is fixed (sourced or cut) or the loop stays open. Medium and low findings are fixed or recorded with a reason.

### What this rubric cannot see

It measures the text, not a reader. It cannot tell whether a real reader pictured the scene or felt talked down to. That needs a test with real readers. It also cannot verify a source it was not given: a critic without the source files can only flag a detail as "unsourced?", not prove it false.

## Before and after (invented example, no real people)

**2D**

> A token is the unit an AI model reads and writes. Usage is billed per token. Longer messages cost more.

**3D**

> A token is the unit an AI model reads and writes, and the unit it bills you for.
>
> Look at it first from your side of the keyboard. You type a sentence. To you it's eight words. To the model it's a handful of small pieces, some whole words, some fragments of words, and each piece is a token.
>
> Now look at it from the meter's side. Picture the electricity meter in a hallway, the little dial that turns when you switch on a lamp. Your message turns the dial once when the model reads it. The answer turns it again while the model writes it. A long question and a long answer turn it further.
>
> And look at it from the end of the month. The bill doesn't list your ideas. It lists a count of pieces in and pieces out. That's why a short, clear request costs less than a long, vague one.

What changed: the definition stayed; the billing clause moved up into it. Three sides were added (the writer, the meter, the bill). The meter is an image and would be marked as one in the source note. No number, event or quote was invented.
